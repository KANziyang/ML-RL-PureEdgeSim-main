\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{subsec:intro-overview}

The transition toward 6G is expected to support ultra-low-latency services, massive device connectivity, and tightly integrated intelligence across communication and computing infrastructures. Under this vision, computation can no longer be treated as a cloud-only service. Instead, latency-sensitive and computation-intensive workloads must be distributed across an edge-cloud continuum in which local devices, nearby edge data centers, and remote cloud servers cooperate under time-varying wireless and computing conditions \cite{saad2020vision, satyanarayanan2017emergence, mao2017survey}.

Within this edge–cloud continuum, wireless resource constraints play a critical role in determining system performance. This report studies a three-tier LOCAL--EDGE--CLOUD architecture in which task offloading is coupled with wireless Physical Resource Block (PRB) allocation. Although 6G standardization is still evolving, schedulable radio resources will remain a fundamental systems constraint in future mobile networks. Accordingly, the uplink is modeled through a PRB-based abstraction inherited from OFDM-based cellular systems such as 5G NR. Under this abstraction, the controller must decide both where a task should execute and how much wireless resource should be reserved for reaching that destination. Offloading to a powerful remote server may reduce computation time while increasing transmission delay and PRB contention, whereas keeping too many tasks local preserves bandwidth but can overload resource-constrained devices. As the number of devices grows, destination choice, radio contention, queueing, mobility, and heterogeneous compute capacity interact to form a high-dimensional, non-stationary control problem that is difficult to handle with static heuristics alone.

To address this problem, the report studies on-policy reinforcement learning for dynamic resource allocation in 6G edge-cloud computing. MAPPO is the primary method, while a shared-policy PPO agent serves as the closest controlled learning baseline under the same environment and dual-action formulation. Rather than assuming in advance that multi-agent modeling is always superior, this report addresses three concrete research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can on-policy reinforcement learning improve QoS and overall system efficiency over heuristic schedulers when execution destination and PRB reservation are optimized jointly in the studied LOCAL--EDGE--CLOUD setting?
    \item Within this joint-control formulation, is adaptive PRB allocation the dominant source of performance gain relative to other MAPPO design choices, such as agent-specific conditioning?
    \item Relative to a shared-policy PPO baseline under the same environment interface, does MAPPO provide a meaningful advantage in deadline-sensitive reliability, and if so, where does that advantage appear?
\end{enumerate}

The main contributions are summarized as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
    \item The joint task-offloading and PRB-allocation problem is formulated as a decentralized partially observable Markov decision process (Dec-POMDP) in which task-generating devices act as decentralized agents.
    \item A MAPPO framework is designed under centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE), combining per-device actors, agent-aware representations, and a centralized critic over the global simulator state.
    \item A shared-policy PPO baseline is implemented under the same environment interface and dual-action design, enabling a controlled comparison with the proposed multi-agent method.
    \item PureEdgeSim is extended into an end-to-end training, inference, and evaluation pipeline with PRB-aware networking, a distance-sensitive wireless model, telemetry logging, and Java--Python co-simulation over a TCP JSON protocol.
    \item The resulting framework is evaluated against heuristic and learning-based baselines using QoS and system-cost metrics, including task success rate, completion latency, energy consumption, and communication-resource usage.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Background}
\label{subsec:intro-background}

\subsubsection{6G and Mobile Edge Computing}

6G visions increasingly place intelligence, communication, and distributed computing within a single end-to-end system. Emerging applications such as immersive media, intelligent transportation, industrial automation, and large-scale IoT require both high-bandwidth connectivity and fast computation close to end users \cite{saad2020vision}. Mobile edge computing addresses this demand by moving computation closer to users and thereby reducing backhaul pressure and end-to-end latency relative to cloud-only execution \cite{satyanarayanan2017emergence, mao2017survey}. In practice, however, edge resources remain limited and heterogeneous, so realistic deployment requires coordination among local devices, edge data centers, and the cloud rather than reliance on any single tier.

This connection between 6G and MEC becomes especially important once radio-resource allocation is taken into account. Even if nearby edge servers are available, a task cannot benefit from offloading unless the wireless link is granted sufficient transmission resources. In practical systems, schedulable uplink radio resources determine the effective bandwidth available to each task and therefore shape transmission delay, queueing pressure, deadline satisfaction, and the usefulness of remote execution itself. Here, these wireless resources are represented through a PRB-based abstraction inspired by current OFDM-based cellular systems. Radio-resource scheduling is therefore not a secondary networking detail, but a central component of intelligent edge-cloud orchestration.

This observation changes the meaning of task offloading itself. Earlier formulations often treat offloading as a destination-selection problem or, at most, as a decision over what fraction of a task should be sent to the edge. In a radio-resource-constrained environment, however, the scheduler must jointly determine where the task will execute and how much wireless resource should be allocated to make that decision effective. Without reasonable PRB allocation, an apparently attractive compute destination can still produce a poor end-to-end service outcome.

\subsubsection{Traditional Task Offloading Methods}

Classical task-offloading strategies often rely on heuristics, static priority rules, convex optimization, or other model-based formulations. These methods can perform well when the environment is simple, stationary, and fully observable. In realistic multi-user systems, however, task arrivals, wireless conditions, queue lengths, mobility, and resource availability evolve continuously, so the assumptions behind static formulations become increasingly restrictive \cite{luo2024survey, robles2023multilayer}.

An additional limitation is that many traditional formulations treat communication delay as a fixed penalty or compress wireless capacity into a coarse scalar term. Once finite radio resources are shared by many devices, this abstraction becomes too weak. A destination that appears optimal under static compute assumptions may become poor when the uplink receives insufficient resources, because transmission time, congestion, and deadline misses can dominate the end-to-end outcome. Conversely, a conservative local or nearby-edge decision may be preferable under radio congestion. The core difficulty is therefore not compute placement alone, but joint communication-and-computation allocation.

\subsubsection{Why Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning}

Reinforcement learning is attractive because it improves decision quality through repeated interaction with a dynamic environment rather than by relying on a fully specified analytical model. For edge-cloud orchestration, however, a single-agent formulation becomes increasingly awkward as the number of devices and feasible actions grows. Devices interact through shared PRBs, shared edge queues, and finite compute resources, so one device's choice can immediately change the feasible and desirable actions for others. This coupling makes the scheduling problem naturally multi-agent rather than merely large-scale.

Recent MEC literature therefore increasingly turns to cooperative MARL, hierarchical MARL, or other distributed learning schemes to coordinate multiple users under shared constraints \cite{lu2020mec_maddpg_sac, ke2022demadrl, sun2023hdmappo, robles2023multilayer}. MARL is particularly suitable here because each device can act on local information at decision time, while training can still exploit system-level information to learn cooperative behavior. In this report, QoS is defined as the user-perceived quality of task service. Operationally, a task is considered to have high QoS when it completes successfully within its latency constraint and consumes only a small fraction of its deadline budget. Accordingly, task success rate is treated as the primary QoS indicator, while completion latency, deadline violations, and other failure-related outcomes provide a more detailed view of deadline-sensitive reliability. Energy usage and communication-resource occupation are treated separately as system-cost metrics that must be balanced against these service-level objectives rather than being folded into QoS in the narrow sense. This distinction is important because a scheduler may improve immediate task completion quality only by consuming excessive energy or PRBs, which would weaken overall system efficiency. This combination of partial observability, shared constraints, and system-level trade-offs makes on-policy MARL a well-motivated modeling choice.

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{subsec:intro-related}

Recent work on intelligent edge orchestration confirms the value of learning-based control, but most MEC studies still simplify the communication side of the problem. Lu et al. combine MADDPG and SAC for server selection and offloaded data amount \cite{lu2020mec_maddpg_sac}. Ke et al. propose DDQN for partial offloading and bandwidth allocation \cite{ke2022demadrl}. Sun and He develop a hierarchical MAPPO scheduler \cite{sun2023hdmappo}, and Aghapour et al. combine DRL-based offloading with swarm-based compute allocation \cite{aghapour2023offloading}. Taken together, these studies show that learning improves orchestration quality, yet they usually optimize destination choice, offloading ratio, or coarse bandwidth variables rather than explicit PRB-level contention. This broader pattern is consistent with the recent survey by Luo and Dai, which notes that latency and energy dominate MEC objectives while PPO- and MAPPO-based formulations remain relatively uncommon \cite{luo2024survey}.

Coordination structure and simulation fidelity remain equally important. Robles-Enciso and Skarmeta use PureEdgeSim to study RL-based coordination across multiple continuum layers \cite{robles2022continuum, robles2023multilayer}, showing the importance of partial observability and guided coordination, but their approach remains tabular and does not model PRB-aware wireless contention. More generally, PureEdgeSim provides a practical basis for heterogeneous edge-cloud experiments \cite{mechalikh2021pureedgesim}, and later efforts such as EISim and ML-RL-PureEdgeSim extend it toward AI-oriented studies \cite{abid2023eisim, mlrlpureedgesimgithub}. However, these frameworks do not provide the bidirectional Java--Python on-policy loop and PRB-aware wireless model required for the present problem.

Method choice is also still open. Yu et al. show that PPO-style methods can be highly competitive in cooperative multi-agent learning when paired with centralized critics and stable optimization \cite{yu2022surprising}. In the wireless domain, Giwa et al. likewise report strong PPO performance for joint power, bandwidth, and scheduling allocation \cite{giwa2025hetnet}. These results make MAPPO and a shared-policy PPO baseline natural candidates for the present setting, especially when the goal is to isolate the value of explicit multi-agent conditioning under one common environment interface.

Against this background, the main research gap is clear. Existing MEC studies rarely combine a cloud-inclusive architecture, explicit PRB reservation, and on-policy learning in one comparable environment; the closest MAPPO-based study \cite{sun2023hdmappo} excludes the cloud tier and does not model explicit PRB reservation. The present report addresses this gap by studying joint execution-destination and PRB-reservation decisions in a customized PureEdgeSim environment with 160 devices and more than 67,000 decisions per episode. This positioning leads directly to the three research questions introduced earlier: whether RL improves QoS and system efficiency over heuristics, whether adaptive PRB control is the dominant design factor, and whether MAPPO provides a meaningful deadline-sensitive reliability advantage over a shared-policy PPO baseline.

\subsection{Report Organization}
\label{subsec:intro-organization}

The remainder of this report is organized as follows. \Secref{sec:simulation} describes the simulation environment, including the PureEdgeSim foundation, the project-specific extensions, and the instantiated experimental configuration. \Secref{sec:system-model} formalizes the system model and problem formulation, covering the network architecture, task model, communication model, computation model, energy model, and Dec-POMDP definition. \Secref{sec:methodology} presents the MAPPO framework, observation and action design, network architecture, reward function, and training algorithm. \Secref{sec:results} reports the experimental evaluation, including standard MAPPO training and evaluation, device-count scalability, ablation studies, a multi-algorithm offline comparison, and the MAPPO--PPO analysis. \Secref{sec:conclusion} summarizes the findings, discusses limitations, and outlines directions for future work.
