\section{Experiment Results}
\label{sec:results}

This chapter reports the experimental evaluation of the proposed MAPPO-based joint offloading and PRB allocation framework introduced in \Secref{sec:methodology}. The results are organized into four parts: (i) standard-setting MAPPO training and evaluation, (ii) a device-count scalability study over 80, 160, 240, and 320 devices, (iii) an ablation study examining agent embeddings and adaptive PRB control, and (iv) an offline comparison against 10 scheduling algorithms under the 160-device setting, with PPO treated as the strongest learning-based baseline within that comparison.

\subsection{MAPPO under the Standard 40-Episode, 30-Minute Setting}
\label{subsec:mappo-standard-setting}

The standard MAPPO experiment uses the default configuration introduced in \Secref{sec:simulation}: a 30-minute simulation horizon, 160 task-generating devices, four edge data centers, one cloud node, and the LOCAL--EDGE--CLOUD architecture. The training run contains 40 episodes, each executed as a full PureEdgeSim episode driven by the Java--Python co-simulation loop.

\paragraph{Training behavior.}
Under this setting, MAPPO converges to a high-quality joint policy. The task success rate rises from \(57.85\%\) in episode 1 to \(99.91\%\) in episode 40. The mean success rate increases from \(75.79\%\) over the first five episodes to \(99.87\%\) over the last five episodes. MAPPO exceeds \(95\%\) success by episode 6 and \(99\%\) by episode 13. Over the same interval, the average total task time decreases from \(5.0108\,\mathrm{s}\) to \(4.6622\,\mathrm{s}\), and delay-induced failures fall from 28,193 to 52. These concurrent improvements indicate that learning improves both reliability and end-to-end execution quality.

The full training trajectory is shown in \Figref{fig:mappo-training-progress}. Beyond the high final success rate, the more important observation is the stable late-stage plateau. Over the last 10 episodes, the mean success rate is \(99.80\%\) with a standard deviation of \(0.131\). The training log records an average simulator wall time of \(227.74\,\mathrm{s}\) per episode, or \(9109.46\,\mathrm{s}\) in total for 40 episodes, which corresponds to approximately 2.53 hours end to end.

\subsection{Performance of MAPPO under Different Numbers of Devices}
\label{subsec:mappo-device-count}

To evaluate the scalability of the proposed MAPPO framework, a device-count ablation study was conducted under the same 40-episode training setting, 30-minute simulation horizon, four edge data centers, and one cloud node. The only variable is the number of task-generating edge devices, which is swept over \(\{80, 160, 240, 320\}\). Each configuration is trained independently from scratch and then evaluated on three seeds (9001, 9002, 9003). The infrastructure topology and all hyperparameters remain identical across configurations.

\paragraph{Training convergence.}
\Figref{fig:ablation-devices-train} shows the episode reward curves for all four device counts. The 80-device and 160-device configurations converge quickly and stably: the 80-device model plateaus around episode 15 at approximately \(150\,\mathrm{K}\) reward, while the 160-device model reaches its plateau near \(292\,\mathrm{K}\) by episode 17. The 240-device configuration exhibits a more turbulent learning trajectory, starting from deeply negative rewards (\(-233\,\mathrm{K}\) in episode 1) and spending the first 10 episodes in a volatile exploration phase before eventually converging to approximately \(420\,\mathrm{K}\) by episode 22. This suggests that 40 episodes are barely sufficient for 240 devices. The 320-device configuration, by contrast, fails to converge entirely: its reward oscillates between \(-35\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(+39\,\mathrm{K}\) throughout all 40 episodes, never establishing a stable positive trend.

\end{figure}

\paragraph{The 320-device collapse: infrastructure saturation.}
The evidence suggests that the 320-device deterioration is driven primarily by infrastructure saturation rather than by ordinary optimization noise. At 320 devices, the simulator generates approximately \(130\,\mathrm{K}\) task decisions per episode, a \(30\%\) increase over the 240-device case. However, the number of delay failures jumps from 451 to 61,899---a \(137\times\) increase---indicating that the system crosses a capacity threshold.

The evidence for infrastructure saturation is visible in the learned policy's behavioral adaptation. \Tabref{tab:ablation-devices-destination} shows how the destination distribution shifts as device count increases. At 80 devices, MAPPO sends roughly \(60\%\) of tasks to the cloud, leveraging its stable bandwidth and ample compute. As device count grows, the cloud fraction steadily decreases: from \(60\%\) at 80 devices to \(52\%\) at 160, \(31\%\) at 240, and only \(12\%\) at 320. Simultaneously, the local execution fraction rises from \(20\%\) to \(51\%\). This shift demonstrates that the policy is actively adapting to cloud congestion by redirecting traffic locally. At 320 devices, however, even with half of all tasks executed locally, the remaining offloaded workload still overwhelms the edge and cloud tiers, resulting in massive queuing delays. The \texttt{average\_real\_total\_time\_s} metric---which includes queuing time---reaches \(26\text{--}36\,\mathrm{s}\) at 320 devices, compared with \(3.2\,\mathrm{s}\) at 80 devices, confirming severe resource contention.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The device-count study therefore suggests that MAPPO remains effective within the capacity envelope of the underlying infrastructure. From 80 to 240 devices, the policy maintains near-perfect task success rates while adaptively shifting its destination distribution to balance load across tiers. At 320 devices, the fixed infrastructure of four edge data centers and one cloud node can no longer absorb the workload within deadline constraints. Scaling beyond this range likely requires additional compute or network capacity rather than only further tuning of the learning algorithm.

\subsection{Ablation Study of MAPPO}
\label{subsec:mappo-ablation}

Two controlled ablations were conducted under the same 40-episode, 30-minute, 160-device setting as the full MAPPO run. \textbf{NoEmbedding} removes the 16-dimensional agent embedding while keeping the same dual-head actor and centralized critic. \textbf{FixedPRB} keeps the destination policy trainable but disables adaptive radio allocation by clamping every non-local action to the fourth PRB bin (index 3), which under the default \(B_{\max}=50\) corresponds to a fixed reservation of \(10\) blocks, or \(20\%\) of the per-task PRB cap; local execution still forces PRB \(=0\). These two variants isolate two different questions: whether explicit agent identity matters, and whether adaptive PRB control matters.

\paragraph{NoEmbedding: controlled but measurable degradation.}
The NoEmbedding model still converges to a strong scheduler, but it exhibits a consistent reliability penalty relative to the full MAPPO policy. During training, success rises from \(57.44\%\) in episode 1 to \(99.69\%\) in episode 40, which is only slightly below the full model's \(99.91\%\). The difference appears mainly in the tail of the failure distribution rather than in coarse routing behavior: the three-seed mean success drops from \(99.9237\%\) to \(99.8148\%\), while mean delay failures increase from \(48\) to \(123\). The mean total time is slightly lower at \(4.4991\,\mathrm{s}\), but this marginal latency gain does not compensate for worse deadline compliance. The mean simulator wall time also drops from \(227.74\,\mathrm{s}\) per episode to \(202.86\,\mathrm{s}\), so the ablation is cheaper to train, but it is not preferable when deadline compliance is the primary objective.

The representative seed-9001 charts in \Figref{fig:noembedding-run-summary}, \Figref{fig:noembedding-reward}, and \Figref{fig:noembedding-actions} show why the degradation is limited rather than severe. The run-summary chart is still dominated by the success bar, and the reward EMA remains high and positive across most of the episode. However, the negative reward spikes are more frequent and the EMA dips more noticeably than in the full MAPPO case, indicating less stable local decision quality. The destination-distribution chart remains almost unchanged, with \(51.78\%\) of tasks still sent to the cloud and \(19.83\%\) kept local. The main behavioral shift appears in the PRB chart: the policy still places most offloaded tasks in the \(1\)-block bin, but compared with the full MAPPO policy it shifts some of the remaining mass from \(10\) blocks to \(20\) blocks, with the \(20\)-block bin rising to \(16.94\%\). This suggests that once agent identity is removed, the shared policy compensates by using slightly more aggressive uplink reservation on difficult decisions.

\paragraph{FixedPRB: structural failure of the joint scheduler.}
The FixedPRB ablation changes the outcome qualitatively. Training improves only from \(55.90\%\) to \(91.52\%\) success across the 40 episodes and plateaus there rather than entering the near-perfect regime. In evaluation, mean success collapses to \(92.3822\%\), mean energy jumps to \(309.2645\,\mathrm{Wh}\), and mean delay failures rise to \(5127.67\). Unlike NoEmbedding, this is not a small regularization effect; it is a direct loss of scheduling capability. The simulator also becomes much faster to run at \(145.41\,\mathrm{s}\) per episode on average, but this speedup is associated with a simpler yet substantially poorer operating regime.

The representative seed-9001 charts in \Figref{fig:fixedprb-run-summary}, \Figref{fig:fixedprb-reward}, and \Figref{fig:fixedprb-actions} reveal the mechanism. The run-summary chart is dominated by the failed-delay bar, and the reward timeline becomes highly oscillatory with dense negative excursions, showing that bad decisions are frequent throughout the episode rather than confined to a few isolated events. The destination distribution also collapses to a two-way split: \(43.37\%\) local and \(42.29\%\) cloud, with the four edge nodes together receiving only \(14.35\%\) of selections. Most decisively, the PRB distribution degenerates to a single point mass, with \(100\%\) of offloaded tasks using \(10\) blocks by construction. Read against the full MAPPO seed-9001 distribution, this is a strong restriction: the learned policy normally uses \(1\) block for most offloads and expands to \(10\) or \(20\) blocks only when needed. Fixing every offloaded task at \(10\) blocks therefore creates a systematic mismatch, being too conservative for hard transfers and too wasteful for easy ones. The result is a system that underuses edge resources, overconsumes mist energy, and accumulates thousands of deadline misses even though fallback, mobility, and network failures remain at zero.

Taken together, the ablations identify adaptive PRB control as the dominant contributor to overall performance. Removing it severely reduces both reliability and energy efficiency. Agent embeddings provide a smaller but consistent benefit: removing them does not break the scheduler, but it increases residual delay failures and pushes the policy toward more aggressive PRB usage. The MAPPO design therefore derives most of its practical value from joint offloading-and-PRB learning, while explicit agent conditioning refines that shared control and delivers an additional reliability gain.

\subsection{Comparison between Reinforcement Learning and Baseline Algorithms}
\label{subsec:rl-vs-baselines}

The offline all-algorithm comparison shows that the reinforcement-learning policies outperform the heuristic baselines under the standard 160-device setting. MAPPO achieves the highest task success rate at \(99.96\%\), followed closely by PPO at \(99.94\%\). The strongest heuristic remains ROUND\_ROBIN at \(90.39\%\), which is still far below both learned policies. These gains are not obtained simply by spending more energy: MAPPO and PPO both use about \(231.3\,\mathrm{Wh}\), which is substantially lower than ROUND\_ROBIN's \(298.26\,\mathrm{Wh}\).

\Tabref{tab:baseline-comparison} lists the main metrics for all 10 algorithms, while \Figref{fig:baseline-comparison} visualizes the same comparison. Several patterns are worth emphasizing. Second, compared with PPO, MAPPO is slightly more reliable and slightly lower in energy, whereas PPO is slightly faster in average total time by \(0.0670\,\mathrm{s}\). Third, CLOUD appears attractive in latency and energy, yet its success rate remains only \(64.64\%\), so it is not a viable scheduling policy when completion reliability matters. Fourth, LOCAL fails mainly because 33,630 tasks cannot be executed under local-only capacity constraints, which highlights the need for coordinated multi-tier offloading.

One limitation should be noted: the PPO run does not preserve per-decision trajectory CSV files, so destination selection and PRB behavior cannot be analyzed at the same granularity as MAPPO. This does not affect the aggregate metrics, but it prevents a fully symmetric behavioral comparison. Taken together with the ablation results in \Secref{subsec:mappo-ablation}, the baseline study suggests that the dominant source of performance gain is reinforcement learning itself, while MAPPO contributes a smaller but consistent reliability gain under deadline pressure. In other words, PPO confirms that on-policy RL is strong in this environment, whereas MAPPO's additional value in the current scenario is that it translates similar average performance into slightly better tail control for multi-terminal coordination. MAPPO therefore remains the central contribution of this report: it achieves near-best aggregate performance while offering the more reliable deadline-sensitive scheduler in the present setting.
